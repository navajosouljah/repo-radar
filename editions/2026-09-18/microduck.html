● What makes it different

Six degrees of freedom

Full 6-DOF articulation gives microduck the workspace and dexterity needed for meaningful manipulation research. Unlike 3-4 DOF hobby arms, it can approach objects from arbitrary angles and execute complex pick-and-place trajectories.

Force-feedback grippers

The gripper provides real-time force sensing, enabling research on contact-rich manipulation - picking up fragile objects, insertion tasks, and force-controlled assembly. This is the feature that separates research hardware from toys.

Imitation learning SDK

Purpose-built Python SDK for imitation learning workflows. Record demonstrations, replay trajectories, and train policies with a clean API. Compatible with popular IL frameworks like ACT, Diffusion Policy, and ALOHA.

Sim-to-real ready

URDF and MuJoCo models included. Train policies in simulation, then deploy directly to the physical arm. The SDK handles the sim-to-real transfer pipeline, including calibration and domain randomization utilities.

$250 bill of materials

Complete BOM with sourcing links. 3D-printed structural parts, off-the-shelf servos, and a standard microcontroller. A university lab can build a fleet of 10 for the price of one commercial research arm.

Dual-arm configurations

Built to work in bimanual setups. Two microduck arms plus cameras create a research station for contact-rich bimanual manipulation - the configuration used in cutting-edge dexterous manipulation research.

Single best feature

Democratizing manipulation research

The real innovation is not any single technical feature - it is the price point. A Franka Emika Panda costs $30,000+. A Universal Robots arm starts at $25,000. Microduck costs $250 and provides enough capability for meaningful research. This means every graduate student, every hobbyist, every lab in a developing country can do real manipulation research. The force-feedback grippers are the specific feature that makes this possible - without them, it would be a toy. With them, it is a research platform.

The robotics access problem

AI manipulation research requires real hardware. Real hardware costs tens of thousands of dollars. This prices out most of the world's researchers.

Before

Research arms cost $25K-$50K. A single Franka Panda or UR5e costs more than many graduate student stipends. Building a multi-arm research station means six-figure equipment budgets. Most labs cannot afford even one.

Hobby arms lack research capability. Sub-$500 robot arms exist, but they lack force sensing, have 3-4 DOF, and use imprecise servos. You cannot do serious manipulation research on hardware that cannot feel what it is gripping.

Sim-only research hits a wall. Simulation is cheap but the sim-to-real gap is real. Policies trained purely in simulation fail on physical hardware in unpredictable ways. You need real hardware to validate your research.

After

$250 for research-grade hardware. Microduck provides 6-DOF articulation and force-feedback grippers at a fraction of commercial arm costs. A lab can build a fleet for the price of one commercial arm.

Purpose-built for AI research. The SDK is designed around imitation learning workflows - record, replay, train, deploy. Compatible with ACT, Diffusion Policy, and other popular frameworks out of the box.

Real sim-to-real pipeline. URDF and MuJoCo models ship with the repo. Train in simulation, deploy on hardware, iterate. The full pipeline is documented and tested.

Build your microduck

From parts order to first teleoperation in a weekend. Total cost: approximately $250.

1

# github.com/pollen-robotics/microduck/blob/main/BOM.md

# 3D print structural parts (STL files in /cad)
# Order servos, controller board, and gripper components
# Total BOM cost: ~$250 USD

The BOM lists every part with sourcing links. 3D printing takes about 12 hours on a standard FDM printer. Servo assembly and wiring is documented with video guides.

2

Assemble and calibrate

# Flash the controller firmware
python -m microduck.firmware flash

# Run the calibration routine
python -m microduck.calibrate

# Test basic motion
python -m microduck.demo wave

Assembly follows a step-by-step guide with photos. Calibration is automated - the script moves each joint through its range and records the servo mapping.

3

Record your first demonstration

# Start teleoperation recording
from microduck import MicroDuck, Recorder

arm = MicroDuck(port="/dev/ttyUSB0")
recorder = Recorder(arm, cameras=[0, 1])

# Teleoperate and record
recorder.start()
# ... move the arm through the task ...
recorder.stop("pick_and_place_demo")

The recorder captures joint positions, gripper force, and camera feeds at 30 Hz. Demonstrations are saved in a format compatible with major imitation learning frameworks.

Research applications

Microduck enables research that was previously locked behind five-figure equipment budgets.

Imitation Learning

Teach robots by showing them. Record human demonstrations of a task, train a policy on the trajectory and visual data, then deploy the learned behavior. Microduck's SDK streamlines this workflow with recording, replay, and training utilities built in.

Sim-to-Real Transfer

Validate simulation policies on real hardware. Train manipulation policies in MuJoCo or Isaac Gym using microduck's URDF model, then deploy to the physical arm. The gap between sim and real is where real research happens.

Classroom Robotics

Hands-on manipulation for every student. A class of 30 students can each have their own arm for $7,500 total. Compare that to $750,000 for commercial arms. Universities can teach contact-rich manipulation as a lab course, not a lecture.

Bimanual Manipulation

Two arms, double the research. Microduck is designed for dual-arm setups. Two arms with cameras create a bimanual manipulation station for research on coordinated grasping, handovers, and assembly tasks.

Watch out for

  • Not industrial grade. Microduck uses hobby servos and 3D-printed parts. Payload capacity, precision, and durability are appropriate for research, not production. Do not expect UR5e specifications at $250.
  • Assembly required. This is a build-it-yourself kit. Budget a weekend for printing, assembly, and calibration. Firmware flashing and servo wiring require basic electronics comfort.
  • Servo limitations. Hobby servos have limited torque, slower response times, and less precise position control than the actuators in commercial arms. Research requiring sub-millimeter precision may hit hardware limits.
  • Community support, not enterprise support. Support comes from GitHub issues and the community. There is no phone line, SLA, or guaranteed response time. Pollen Robotics provides guidance but this is an open-source project, not a product.
