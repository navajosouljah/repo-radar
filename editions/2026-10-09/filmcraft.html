How does it work?

Your problem

You need to cut a video - trim clips, add transitions, grade the color, export an MP4 - but Premiere costs $23 a month and the free alternatives feel limited.

You have 20 minutes of footage and need a polished 3-minute video with titles, transitions and color correction.

When it kicks in

You open FilmCraft and import your footage, or point an AI agent at it through its command interface.

Import your video files and start cutting - the timeline uses the same three-point editing and trim tools Premiere users know.

You give it

Your video and audio files: H.264, HEVC, ProRes, VP9, AV1, DNxHD and more - decoded by FilmCraft's own codecs, not a third-party library.

Drop in MP4 files from your phone, ProRes files from a camera, or MXF files from a broadcast workflow.

It does

It gives you a full editing workspace with a multi-track timeline, color grading, effects and titling.

  1. Cut and arrange clips on a multi-track timeline with three-point editing, ripple/roll/slip/slide trims, and J/K/L playback
  2. Grade the color with a Lumetri-style panel: color wheels, curves, HSL secondaries and scopes
  3. Add from about 55 video effects and 30 transitions, with keyframes and Bezier interpolation
  4. Export as H.264 MP4, ProRes, MXF or image sequences - with broadcast-standard loudness metering

The README demo: import footage, add a title track, grade with color wheels, and export an H.264 MP4.

You get

A finished video file, exported on your own computer.

  • Exported videoAn H.264 MP4 ready to upload, or a ProRes file for further editing
  • Subtitle filesSRT, WebVTT or broadcast caption formats (SCC, CEA-608/708), exported alongside the video
  • Project interchangeExport as Final Cut Pro XML, OpenTimelineIO, EDL or AAF to move between editors

What changes for me?

Without it

  • Cutting a video with color grading and proper export means paying Adobe $23 a month - or using a free editor with fewer codec and format options.
  • Moving a project between editors means hoping the interchange format works. Many free editors don't support broadcast formats.

With it

  • A full timeline editor with Premiere-style color grading, on your machine, for free - including its own H.264, HEVC and ProRes codecs.
  • Export your project as Final Cut Pro XML, OpenTimelineIO or EDL and move it to another editor if you outgrow this one.

How hard is it to try?

Effort

5 minutesAbout an hourNeeds a developer

Should I?

Great if you...

  • You know Premiere's timeline workflow and want a free alternative that speaks the same language - same trims, same shortcuts, same color panel
  • You need broadcast-standard features (loudness metering, drop-frame timecode, caption formats) in a free editor

Skip it if...

  • You need a stable editor for paid work today - FilmCraft estimates itself at 50-60% readiness and its testing on Windows and Linux is limited
  • You rely on Premiere plugins (VST3, AU, OpenFX) - FilmCraft has no plugin hosting yet

Instead, you could look at

  • DaVinci ResolveThe industry-standard free editor with professional color grading. Far more mature, but proprietary and a much larger install.
  • KdenliveA mature free video editor on Linux, Windows and Mac. Stable and well-tested, with a different interface.

Is it safe?

PASSED

  • Searches for CVEs, vulnerabilities, malware and scams4 results read, including the national CVE database: nothing found
  • Are the stars earned?7.6K stars, 30 contributors, 842 commits. findarepo: looks organic

Watch out for

  • About 50-60% ready for real work. The project's own estimate. Speed on large footage, plugin hosting and broad camera-file support are the main gaps.
  • Part of the ArtCraft suite by storytold. Same maker as PhotoCraft, LightCraft, PdfCraft and ArtCraft. Early alpha, built by one developer with AI.
  • No FFmpeg inside, for better or worse. FilmCraft implements its own H.264, HEVC, ProRes, VP9 and AV1 codecs. That removes a dependency but means codec support is narrower and less battle-tested than FFmpeg's.
  • Windows and Linux get less testing. The README notes that macOS receives the most testing. Expect more rough edges on other platforms.
