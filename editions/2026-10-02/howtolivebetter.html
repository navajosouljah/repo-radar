How does it work?

Your problem

Life advice is everywhere, but most of it is unsourced opinions. When you actually need to make a decision - about health, money, insurance, or legal rights - you can't tell what's evidence-based and what's a guess.

A friend tells you to take a supplement. Is there real evidence it works? How much does it cost per year? What's the measured benefit?

When it kicks in

You open the website and search or filter by topic, evidence grade, cost or benefit type.

Search 'vitamin D' or filter for 'longevity' + 'A-grade evidence' + 'under $50/year' to see only the strongest, cheapest recommendations.

You give it

A question about your life: health, money, legal rights, insurance, emergencies, relationships, career, or going abroad.

34 topics: longevity, disease prevention, first aid, saving money, legal red lines, unemployment, health insurance, parenting, entrepreneurship, skills for emigrating, and more.

It does

It shows you the recommendations that match, each with three things: what it costs, what you get, and how strong the evidence is.

  1. Each recommendation lists the cost (money, time, effort) and the measured benefit (mortality reduction, savings in dollars, time gained)
  2. Each one carries an evidence grade: A (peer-reviewed journal papers or official government documents), B (multiple credible sources), or C (single credible source)
  3. Every claim links to the original source - a journal paper, a government regulation, or an official document - so you can check it yourself

One recommendation: 'Get a flu shot every year. Cost: about $30 and 30 minutes. Benefit: 40-60% reduction in flu risk. Evidence: A (CDC, multiple clinical trials).' With links to each study.

You get

A short list of the highest-value actions for your situation, each one sourced.

  • Ranked recommendations by cost-effectivenessThe cheapest actions with the biggest proven benefits, sorted so you do the high-value ones first
  • Source links you can verify1,539 links to journal papers and official documents - not blog posts or opinions
  • Downloadable offline versionsPDF, EPUB, or a single HTML file you can keep on your phone for reference anywhere

What changes for me?

Without it

  • Life advice comes from social media, friends, and articles that rarely cite sources or quantify the benefit.
  • Finding the highest-value thing to do for your health or money means reading dozens of studies, if you even know where to look.

With it

  • One searchable guide with 650 recommendations, each graded by evidence quality and showing its exact cost and payoff.
  • Every claim links to the original source - journal papers and official documents, not opinions - so you can verify anything in seconds.

Should I?

Great if you...

  • You want a single place to look up evidence-based life advice, ranked by cost-effectiveness, without wading through unsourced opinions
  • You value being able to verify claims yourself by following source links to the original research

Skip it if...

  • You read only English - the guide is primarily in Chinese, with English and other translations available but less complete
  • You want personalized medical or financial advice - this is a general reference, not a professional consultation

Instead, you could look at

  • UpToDate (for health)The gold standard for evidence-based medical guidance, used by doctors. Subscription-only and written for clinicians, not for a general audience.
  • 80,000 Hours (for career)Evidence-based career advice from the effective altruism community. Narrower scope (careers only) but deeper on that one topic.

Watch out for

  • Primarily in Chinese. English and other translations exist but may lag behind the original. If you don't read Chinese, some recommendations may be region-specific (Chinese law, Chinese insurance systems).
  • Not medical or legal advice. It's a curated reference, not a professional consultation. The evidence grades are the author's own assessment.
  • 25 days old, one main author. The guide is comprehensive (650 items, 1,539 sources), but it hasn't been independently peer-reviewed as a whole.
  • Some recommendations may be China-specific. Legal red lines, insurance, and employment rights sections reference Chinese law and systems.
